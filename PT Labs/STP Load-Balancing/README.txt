This topology was built by Jeremy's IT Lab, while I completed the configuration.

The focal point of this topology for me was the necessity of Spanning-Tree given the multiple redundant links in the network. Without STP, broadcast storms would be inevitable in this setup. Any unknown unicast or broadcast traffic would continue looping through the network, with switches flooding frames to switches that have redundant links leading back to them.

Most modern network switching configurations now use L3 switches and routed links between those to avoid STP altogether beyond the access level. IP routing has a built in TTL value to avoid loops, and routed traffic isn't moving beyond the broadcast domains of its links anyway. Spanning-Tree isn't preferred because it will necessarily block one of your redundant links, effectively leaving it unused unless there is a rare failure of a different link. All the load would be on the active links, with none distributed to the blockign ones. Making those into redundant links L3 enables you to use something like OSPF or EIGRP for load-balancing across the links; neither is just completely disabled.

However, this lab taught me about how PVST+ did improve upon classic STP in the sense that it allows you to load-balance to an extent. By setting different root bridges in different VLANs, the way STP converges and chooses blocking links will change per-VLAN. In this topology, SW1 is the root bridge in VLAN1, and SW2 is the root in VLAN2. 

That means, for example, that in VLAN1, SW4's F0/2 interface (cost 19) will be root with F0/1's link (cost 38) blocking, and in VLAN2 F0/1 is root and F0/2's link is blocking. So, both links are active in different VLANs, meaning that the redundant links aren't just unused emergency failovers, but actually active in the topology. In the event of a link going down, Spanning-Tree would reconverge, using the redundant link with the greater cost. So, the topology includes failover redundancy while maintaining all links as active, at least in certain VLANs.

My last note is that the access ports of SW3 and SW4 are configured to use PortFast and BPDUGuard, which often work in tandem. PortFast eliminates the Listening & Learning link states when a device connects to an access port, which by default are 15 seconds each. The L & L states are used to listen for BPDUs and learn MACs, determining what the port's STP role should be once the 30 seconds elapses. With PortFast, end devices can connect immediately to the network without this convergence period, because they shouldn't be connected to anything that could cause broadcast loops.

BPDUGuard works with PortFast to prevent unintended STP convergence with anything a PortFast-enabled switchport is connected to. If the port receives a BPDU, it's immediately err-disabled.
